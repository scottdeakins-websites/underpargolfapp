Most golfers are taught the **clock system**: shorten your backswing to 9 or 10 o'clock to hit it shorter. It sounds precise. Under pressure, it isn't. Nobody can feel the difference between 9:00 and 9:30 on the 18th hole. And on short shots, a long backswing stores more energy than you need, so you either fly it long or decelerate and chunk it.

There's a simpler way: **keep the swing the same and change the grip.**

## The choke-down method

Hit each wedge three ways, with the same full, smooth swing:

1. Normal grip
2. Choked down 1 inch
3. Choked down 2 inches

Record the **carry** for each. Use a launch monitor if you have one, or pace it off. Here's an example for a pitching wedge:

| Pitching wedge | Carry |
|---|---|
| Full grip | 134 yds |
| 1 in. down | 127 yds |
| 2 in. down | 120 yds |

Do this for every wedge and you'll have a chart that fills the gaps between clubs with shots you've actually measured. Write it on a card and keep it in your bag.

## Inside 60 yards

For shorter shots, set up for a descending strike: narrower stance, choke down on your sand or lob wedge, and about **70% of your weight on your lead foot**. Shorten the backswing and *accelerate* through the ball. Deceleration is what causes chunks and skulls.

## Club choice by pin position

| Pin | Play |
|---|---|
| Front | The shorter wedge, hit firmly for more spin |
| Middle, into the wind | More club, choked down: a knockdown |
| Middle, downwind | Less club, and let it release |
| Back | The longer club as a [flighted wedge](/blogs/full-swing-guide-from-scratch-golf/the-art-of-hitting-flighted-wedges), landing mid-green and rolling back |

## The one rule

Whatever the shot: accelerate through the ball, keep your weight forward, and let the club and grip length control the distance. Then test your chart in the full-wedge block of the [1-hour practice session](/blogs/the-blog/best-1-hour-golf-practice-session).
